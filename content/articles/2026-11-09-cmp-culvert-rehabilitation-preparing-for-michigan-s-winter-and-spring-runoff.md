Michigan municipalities, county road commissions, and transportation agencies rely on corrugated metal pipe (CMP) culverts to maintain drainage and protect roadways. As these structures age, corrosion, material loss, and other forms of deterioration can create structural and maintenance challenges, particularly when culverts are located beneath active roads. 

Michigan's harsh winters bring additional challenges, while spring snowmelt and heavy rainfall increase runoff and place greater demands on aging drainage infrastructure. Addressing existing deterioration before these seasonal conditions intensify can help maintain culvert performance and reduce the risk of more extensive damage. 

For infrastructure owners, the challenge is finding a solution that restores structural performance without the cost and disruption of traditional excavation and replacement.

## Common Causes and Signs of CMP Culvert Deterioration 

Corrugated metal pipe culverts are exposed to ongoing environmental and structural stresses that can affect their performance over time. Common deterioration challenges include: 

- Corrosion and rust: Prolonged exposure to moisture and corrosive conditions can weaken pipe walls, leading to rust, pitting, and thinning. 
- Invert deterioration: Corrosion and abrasion from flowing water and sediment can deteriorate the bottom of the culvert, leading to material loss and reduced structural integrity. 
- Deformation: Traffic loads, soil pressure, and inadequate support can cause flattening, buckling, or changes in pipe alignment. 
- Water infiltration and joint separation: Damaged joints and openings can allow groundwater infiltration and soil migration, potentially creating voids around the culvert. 
- Material loss and perforations: Advanced corrosion and abrasion can create holes or missing sections of metal, compromising the culvert's ability to support surrounding soil and traffic loads. 

While the extent of deterioration varies, these conditions can affect a culvert's structural performance and ability to convey stormwater. Identifying damage early allows infrastructure owners to evaluate rehabilitation options before deterioration becomes more extensive. 

## Preparing Michigan's CMP Culverts for Winter and Spring Runoff 

Fall offers a valuable opportunity to address culvert concerns before Michigan's winter weather sets in. With freezing temperatures ahead and heavier runoff expected during the spring thaw, evaluating aging culverts now can help municipalities and county road commissions plan repairs and rehabilitation projects before seasonal conditions become more challenging. 

Winter and spring bring several conditions that can affect CMP culvert performance: 

- Ice and debris blockages: Snow, ice, and accumulated debris can restrict culvert openings, limiting drainage capacity and increasing the risk of upstream flooding. 
- Freeze-thaw conditions: Repeated freezing and thawing can aggravate existing damage, particularly around deteriorated joints and surrounding soils. 
- Corrosive runoff: Road salts and deicing chemicals carried by runoff can accelerate corrosion in exposed metal, particularly along deteriorated culvert inverts. 
- Increased hydraulic demands: Rapid snowmelt and heavy rainfall can place greater demands on drainage systems. When culverts are already damaged or restricted, water may back up, overflow onto roadways, or contribute to erosion around the structure. 

Routine maintenance, such as clearing debris and checking drainage conditions, can help keep culverts functioning through seasonal changes. However, corrosion, material loss, and other structural deficiencies may require more extensive rehabilitation to restore structural integrity. 

For infrastructure owners, fall inspections provide an opportunity to identify vulnerable culverts, address drainage restrictions, and prioritize rehabilitation projects before winter conditions complicate repairs and spring runoff places additional demands on aging infrastructure.
